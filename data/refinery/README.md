Refinery crude blending and production planning model, generated by
`scripts/refinery_model.py`.

Why it exists: the problem statement names refinery scheduling, crude blending
and production planning as expected data, and MRPL is the proposing
organisation. Every public benchmark instance we had was a 0/+-1 combinatorial
LP - nothing that resembles the model a refinery actually runs.

What it is: buy crudes, charge them to the CDU, take fixed cut yields, route each
stream straight to the blender or through a hydrotreater or the FCC, blend into
finished products subject to sulphur specifications, sell against demand, carry
inventory between periods. Maximise margin.

Two modelling choices are worth stating plainly, because a chemical engineer
will ask about both:

  Blending is linear. True blending is bilinear - the pooling problem, and
  nonconvex. Streams are kept separate down to the blender rather than pooled,
  which keeps the sulphur balance linear and exact. Pooling first is what makes
  the problem nonconvex, and a piecewise-linear MILP relaxation of that is the
  standard next step.

  Units are left as a refinery would state them: barrels, rupees per barrel,
  sulphur in ppm. That is what makes a planning model badly scaled, with a
  coefficient range of about 4e5 here, and it is the property the public
  combinatorial instances lack.

Generate:

    python3 scripts/refinery_model.py --periods=12 --crudes=8 \
        --out=data/refinery/refinery.mps

Sizes, and agreement with HiGHS at a relative tolerance of 1e-8:

    periods  crudes    rows    cols     nonzeros   agreement
          4       4     252     736        2,172     6.3e-10
         12       8   1,380   4,368       12,952     1.7e-08
         52       8   5,980  18,928       56,152     7.1e-09

The 12-period solution runs the CDU at 100% of capacity and the hydrotreater at
100%, with the FCC at 27.5%. It blends four crudes including a mid-sour one at
13,800 ppm, because hydrotreating makes that crude usable - which is the central
economic trade in refinery planning. The sourest and cheapest crudes stay out of
the slate because hydrotreater capacity, not crude price, is the binding
constraint. That is the kind of conclusion a planning model exists to produce.
